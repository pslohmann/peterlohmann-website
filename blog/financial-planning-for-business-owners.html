Financial Planning for Business Owners: Why My Advisor Barely Talks About Net Worth

When I started working with my financial advisor, I figured I’d already know most of what he told me. I’d been handling my own Roth IRAs and 401(k)s for years, and I knew more about tax strategy than 95% of my friends.

I knew way less than I thought. Starting a few years earlier could have saved me tens, if not hundreds, of thousands of dollars.

That advisor is Thomas Kopelman, co-founder of All Street Wealth. He’s handled my financial planning for several years. I brought him on the podcast to talk about financial planning for business owners, starting with something that surprised me: we almost never talk about my net worth.

Net worth is a bad scoreboard for business owners

Thomas does a net worth check about once a year. The rest of the time, we talk about cash: what came in, what goes to taxes, what gets invested, and what’s left.

For a business owner, net worth is mostly guesswork. Your biggest assets are a private company and some real estate, and nobody has offered you a check for either. You can tell yourself the business is worth $2 million and feel great about it in a year when you spent more than you made.

I’ve come around to this. After selling a few properties myself and watching friends try to sell their businesses, I’m a lot more conservative about what my own companies and properties are worth.

Thomas would rather track what you control: your investment rate, the debt you paid down, the tax moves you made. A strong market year can push your net worth up while you make bad decisions with your cash.

He also called net worth “an ego game.” Ask people their goal and they’ll name a round number like $20 million, even if they spend $300,000 a year and don’t need $10 million. His wealthiest clients are worth around $300 million, and his take is blunt: “Nobody feels any different at the next net worth level.”

He admitted he does it too. He told himself he’d be happy at $1 million in revenue, then $2 million. Then he signed four great clients in a week, lost two bad fits, and it still felt like a loss. (Loss aversion is a hell of a thing.)

Spending money is a skill too

A lot of Thomas’s clients are first-generation wealth. They grew up learning to save and buy only what they needed, and that wiring stays put after the income shows up. One client’s family is worth about $80 million, and they’ve never been able to buy clothes anywhere nicer than Target.

So a big part of his work is helping people spend more. His process: start with yearly income, subtract taxes and expenses, set aside what needs to get invested, and look at the surplus. If the backyard project fits in the surplus, do the backyard project.

He treats one-time expenses differently from recurring ones. A $60,000-a-year country club or private school tuition raises your baseline for good.

The other side is harder. Thomas says at least 75% of the clients he’s lost over his career had spending problems, and he doesn’t think anyone can fix that for them. The pressure usually comes from their own circle. When five of your friends are building in the same golf course community, saying no gets hard.

Liquidity comes before the next property

Property managers will recognize this one. Thomas says the clients in the worst shape, even when they don’t know it yet, are often the ones who own nothing but real estate. Twenty properties, no cash, no brokerage account. A couple of vacancies and a big repair later, they’re scrambling. (I can tell you who they call when that happens: their property manager.)

He calls liquidity maybe the most important part of a financial life, and he sizes the emergency fund to how risky your income is. Thomas would have to lose about 80% of his clients before he couldn’t cover his living expenses, so he keeps a small cash cushion. A solo consultant with one big client might need a year of expenses in cash.

A taxable brokerage account changes that math. You can sell during business hours or borrow against it, and the bigger it gets, the smaller your emergency fund can be. When we recorded, Thomas said his clients could get margin loans at 5% to 6%, compared to around 8% for a HELOC. I like this for buying property: borrow against the account, pay cash, and get the mortgage afterward.

Tax planning for small business owners starts with knowing the number

The most common problem Thomas sees with business owners is that they have no idea what they’ll owe. Some hoard cash all year. Others pull it out of the business and panic when the April bill and the Q1 estimate land.

His fix: update the tax projection at every meeting (three or four times a year), know your quarterly payments and safe harbor, and distribute surplus cash once you know what’s left. Most CPAs spend February through October filing returns, so planning gets squeezed into December, if it happens at all.

Once you know the number, strategy gets easier:

  • Entity election. For a profitable PM company, an S-corp can cut self-employment taxes, and you can pay state taxes through the business. PM companies tend to be wage-heavy, so your wage decisions also affect how much of the 20% qualified business income deduction you get.
  • Timing. Buying two properties in one year can push you down into the 10% bracket, so the second property’s depreciation saves you very little now and can cost you more in recapture later. Spreading them across two years often comes out ahead.
  • Capital gains. Almost none of Thomas’s clients pay the 15% long-term rate people assume. Most are at 23.8% or higher, and his California clients are close to 37%. Tax loss harvesting builds up losses you can use against a future sale. I started about a year and a half ago, and I got to use those losses when I sold a property recently.

And don’t spend money just to save tax. In Thomas’s words: “Buying trucks at year end to save on taxes is amazing when you need trucks. When you don’t need trucks, it is just stupid.”

I don’t mind paying taxes in a year I’ve done well, and Thomas agrees: “The goal is not to pay zero in taxes.”

What I’d look for in a financial advisor (and what PMs can steal)

Thomas gave three things to look for:

  • They own a business (or have). He says every business owner he works with fears it’ll all come crashing down, no matter how successful. An advisor who’s never felt that will struggle to guide you through it.
  • They ask for your tax return. If they don’t, he says, “you really don’t have an advisor.”
  • They have a process and reach out to you. He meets clients in three set review seasons a year. One new client worth hundreds of millions says his Goldman advisor never once reached out.

Thomas’s calls made me rethink how property managers work with owners. His team emails me a month or two ahead for documents, spends four to five hours prepping each call, and shows up with an agenda. Most owners treat their property manager like a glorified assistant and only call when something breaks. We could ask for the mortgage statement and last year’s property tax bill before a review call and come in with a plan. Thomas described the relationship he wants with clients as “a thought partner that’s at the table with you.”
