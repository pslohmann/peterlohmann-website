How to Grow a Property Management Company Without Marketing to Owners

Most property managers I know grow the same way: ads, referrals, maybe a BDM working the phones. Chris Clothier's family grew REI Nation to roughly 8,000 doors without ever hiring a business development manager. The company acquires single-family homes, fixes them up, sells each one to an investor, and stays on as the manager for years afterward.

If you've wondered whether you can grow a property management company without marketing to owners, Chris's model is the clearest example I've seen. It also comes with tradeoffs most of us would never accept, so I wanted to hear how the pieces fit together.

A Buy Box Narrow Enough to Turn Away Half Your Leads

REI Nation buys one product: median-priced single-family homes in the 11 Southeast cities where it operates. No multifamily, no luxury, no short-term rentals, and nothing in the most challenged pockets of a market. Chris compared it to making a cheeseburger at any McDonald's in the country. The materials, repairs, rents, and management all look about the same from house to house.

That discipline costs them deals. Chris told me they get about 500 interested buyers a month. Roughly half can't qualify because they want owner financing or a partner to bring the money. Most of the rest have the cash but want the cheapest homes in the market, usually because they live somewhere expensive and a $100K three-bed looks like a steal.

Chris walked through the math those buyers get wrong. With financing, the $100K house feels safer because you only put $20,000 to $25,000 down, versus $50,000 on a $200,000 house. But the $200K house holds its value and stays occupied. The cheap one turns over constantly, so you can lose your entire down payment on the "low-risk" deal. His summary of those streets: "the local Memphis investors won't drive down" them.

I hear the same pitch in Columbus all the time. When an out-of-state investor sends me an $80,000 listing that's sat for three months and circulated on every email list in Ohio, I want to ask why none of the local investors bought it.

Chris figures he could make $10,000 to $20,000 per transaction selling those homes, and move 100 a month. He won't. Every dollar of that profit would go to hiring people and burning time to manage the fallout, and most of REI Nation's growth comes from existing clients buying again.

When You Sell the House, You Own the Outcome

The biggest fear for an investor buying far from home is not knowing what's going on with a property they can't see. REI Nation's answer is that one company finds the house, renovates it, and manages it. When something breaks, the owner never gets bounced between the flipper and the property manager, each blaming the other.

That promise only works if the renovations hold up. Every home gets the same standardized scope list, and the team decides item by item what to replace. Chris estimated they swap out 50% to 60% of water heaters. Then every sale comes with a one-year warranty, whether or not they touched the failed item. As Chris put it, "if you're gonna offer that type of warranty, you better be really, really good at what you're doing... or you'll go out of business."

The data impressed me most. REI Nation has close to 20 years of monthly and quarterly reporting, with renovation costs priced down to copper and nails. They also track the days it takes an owner to approve a move-out list, then the days to start work, finish it, market the home, and re-rent it. Performance data lives in Salesforce, property data in Propertyware, and all of it now feeds a data warehouse they can query with AI by market, year, or quarter. Chris's phrase for the philosophy is "inspect what you expect."

A Dedicated Team for Monthly Owner Calls

REI Nation has more than 3,000 owners, and a dedicated customer service team phones each owner once a month. Eleven people handle roughly 300 owners each. Clients with five or more homes (what they call the WOW Group) get a rep with closer to 100 clients. These reps don't buy, sell, or manage anything. They call to say rent came in and nothing's wrong, and they take notes. If an owner mentions his wife's birthday, a restaurant gift card shows up the following year.

"We identified the biggest fear that investors have about owning property and having a property manager is communication," Chris said. The payoff comes on the bad day. When a water heater fails, the owner already knows the voice on the phone, and that call opens with what the team has already done to fix it.

The result: REI Nation almost never loses an owner to another management company. The real threat is owners who should sell. Chris said even a great renovation lasts seven to ten years before capex starts piling up, so they buy homes back from investors or broker investor-to-investor sales at a discount. The seller can 1031 into a newer property, the buyer gets a deal, and the management stays put.

Why the Profit Engine Flipped, and What Comes Next

For roughly the first decade, the sales side drove REI Nation's profits. Once the portfolio reached four or five thousand homes, management revenue outgrew anything the flipping business could produce. At their peak they sold 100 homes a month, and they did about 1,000 in 2022. Today it's closer to 40 a month, or 400 to 450 for the year.

Chris also expects to lose about 400 homes this year to owners who want to maximize their return on the open market. So buying and selling now mostly keeps them from shrinking. To get back to 1,000 new doors a year, they need another 500 from somewhere else: third-party management, which they've never offered, and acquisitions.

He has a clear filter for acquisitions. He doesn't want a company that manages "anything for anybody anywhere." He wants well-run single-family operators with low turnover. REI Nation uses two-year leases, gets 82% of residents to renew the first time, and sees about 78% renew again after that.

The angle most buyers can't match: every acquired company comes with owners who could become REI Nation investors, and every new market gives REI Nation's own investors somewhere new to buy. Chris estimated about 40% of his investors (around 1,200) plan to add a property within the next couple of years.

Why More Operators Haven't Copied This

I asked Chris the obvious question. If the model works this well, why hasn't everyone done it? His answer: risk and hard work. At one point his family went to bed every night with over $80 million in debt to carry inventory. When two partners from an Atlanta PM company spent a day shadowing REI Nation, they told Chris they had zero desire to work as hard as his team does.

What stuck with me most was the discipline. Chris says yes to every idea, his younger brother says no to almost all of them, and their dad (still CEO in his mid-70s) breaks the tie. Out of a hundred ideas, they might pursue one. They held their ground for nearly two years negotiating a deal with a fund partner because they refused to rebuild their operation around one client's wish list.

I talk to property managers every week who say yes to everything, sometimes for the money and sometimes because they feel obligated to help. Saying yes to everyone and executing poorly doesn't help anybody. You don't need to buy and renovate houses to borrow Chris's playbook. Pick your product, say no to everything outside it, and talk to your owners before they have a reason to call you.
